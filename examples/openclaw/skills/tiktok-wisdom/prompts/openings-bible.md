# Dr Non's openings bible — the 8 shapes that aren't a cliché

> Mined from 50 blog posts (Day 78 → Day 126 + the 100-days book). Use this when drafting podcast scripts, wisdom videos, or any first-person prose in Dr Non's voice. **Do not default to "I remember standing/sitting/listening/taking the bus."** That's a Hollywood-LLM cliché, not Dr Non's voice.
>
> **Forking this skill?** This file is Dr Non's catalogue. Replace it with your own — mine your own corpus the same way (extract the first sentence of every blog/podcast/video script you've published, group them into shapes, list 4-5 examples per shape, write the anti-patterns YOUR LLM keeps falling into). The 8-shape ROTATION rule is universal; the specific shapes will be yours.

---

## Pick from these 8 shapes. Rotate. Don't reuse the same shape two pieces in a row.

### Shape 1 — People-ask-me + the question (his most reliable opener)

The setup is always: someone outside Dr Non's head has asked him something specific. Then he repeats the question out loud, then he answers it directly. This anchors the piece in social interaction, not introspection.

Examples from the blog:

> "People ask me all the time — 'Non, you travel the world giving talks, you're always on panels with people in suits quoting McKinsey and the UN, you seem so confident — how?'" — *Day 123: Confidence Is Built by Failing*

> "People often ask me, 'Non, how do you get so much done?'" — *Day 122: Finding Flow*

> "People look at my resume — MIT, Oxford, Harvard — and they ask, 'How the hell did you pull that off, Non?' They expect me to say I had rich parents…" — *Day 121: Free Rides*

> "People keep asking me." — *Day 120: Daniel Kahneman*

**When to use:** When the topic is a question you've been asked publicly. When the listener might be wondering the same thing. When you want to feel approachable, not preachy.

**Why it works:** It's social proof + anti-monologue. You're not lecturing; you're answering.

---

### Shape 2 — Confession of vulnerability

Open with the moment the floor fell out. Not as drama — as fact, in a tone of "this happened, here's what I learned."

> "In my darkest hours, during which I was choosing between living and dying, I found the perfect method to help me make such a decision — travel." — *Day 111: How 'Light and Cheap' Travel Helped Me Escape Death*

> "When my father passed away in 2010 — the black swan event of my life — I was left with nothing much for which to live." — *Day 81: The Power of Now*

> "First, I can't tell you how happy I am to be alive right now to write this post." — *Day 108: How I Got Out of the Darkest Time*

> "It's one of those moments in life where you'd say 'enough is enough,' leave a full-time job, and travel the world." — *Day 110: 2 Years of Traveling the World*

**When to use:** When the lesson came from genuine pain. When you're writing about resilience, motivation, depression, or radical change.

**Why it works:** Earns the right to teach. You can't sermonise about coming back from darkness if the reader doesn't trust that you've been there.

**WARNING:** Use sparingly. Once or twice a month, max. Confession is high-impact; over-used, it becomes performance.

---

### Shape 3 — You-just-saw-something (concrete recent stimulus)

Something happened in the world THIS WEEK. A scrolling moment. A podcast you heard. An email that arrived. The piece is your in-real-time reaction.

> "So, I'm scrolling X a couple days ago, and this comedian's up on stage, killing it." — *Day 124: The Only Thing That Matters*

> "I recently received an email from a former colleague." — *Day 107: When Things Get Rough, Do This*

> "I was listening to one of my favorite podcast NPR The Hidden Brain the other day." — *Day 91: In Praise of Checklists*

> "I was about to go to bed when the idea of this blog post came to mind!" — *Day 86: On Design Thinking — Dealing with Criticisms*

**When to use:** When the topic is current. When the lesson came from a specific, datable event. When you want to feel alive and timely, not eternal.

**Why it works:** Locates the piece in time. The reader can picture *you*, scrolling, listening, just-now-noticing.

---

### Shape 4 — Quote landed back at the reader

Open with someone else's exact words. Then reveal what those words did to you.

> "'I am so glad to have met this version of you.' These 11 words have gotten me thinking long and hard about how I have come from being no use to no one…" — *Day 109: The Fact, Key, and Action*

> "'A famous Zen monk once said…' (and I paraphrase this from Eckhart Tolle)…" — *Day 99: Why Reading News*

**When to use:** When a single sentence — yours or someone else's — became the seed of an idea. When you want the piece to feel like it's circling something specific.

**Why it works:** The quote lands as a punch in the gut before the reader has time to put up defenses. Then you unpack.

**Variant:** Open with a date + a phrase. Example: *"December 31, 2020. This year marks a very important milestone in my life…"* (Day 113).

---

### Shape 5 — Aphorism / declaration

A single sentence that states a position. No setup. The reader has to come along or get off.

> "We may have suffered more in our own imagination than in reality." — *Day 105: What I've Learned Lately*

> "Freedom is one of the most difficult topics." — *Day 93: On Freedom*

> "Living in the world as hard and chaotic as it is today, it's not enough just to be flexible and resilient — we need to be anti-fragile." — *Day 87: The Anti-Fragile Manifesto*

> "If I were to write my own list of what I should have known before turning 20, the top of the list would have been Stoicism." — *Day 84: On Design Thinking — Stoicism*

**When to use:** When the piece is essay-shaped, not narrative. When the topic is abstract enough that anchoring in scene would dilute it. When you've earned the right to declare without proof — through prior posts or because the audience already knows you.

**Why it works:** Sets a frame. The rest of the piece is then the proof.

### Shape 6 — Buddhist / Taoist parable (yours, you don't say "this is a parable")

Open with a story from Buddhist or Taoist or Stoic tradition. Don't introduce it as a parable — just tell it. Reveal the lesson at the end.

> "A beautiful girl in the village was pregnant." — *Day 101: How to Deal with Unpleasant Events* (the Hakuin "Is that so?" parable)

> "Demanding nothing in return for his kindness, the sage eventually obtains everything; The sage does not accumulate things, Yet the more he gives to others, the more he has." — *Day 104: Why We Should "Stop Trying"* (Tao Te Ching)

> "Once upon a time, a wise man I spoke to…" — *Day 97: Problematizing Time*

**When to use:** When the lesson is universal AND you have a specific tradition's text that names it precisely. When you want to feel less Western, less industrial, more contemplative.

**Why it works:** Parables bypass the reader's analytical defences. They hear a story; they extract the meaning themselves; they own the conclusion.

**WARNING:** Only use parables you actually know. Don't invent one. The voice should sound like *you remembered this*, not like *I generated this*.

---

### Shape 7 — Meta-acknowledgment / single-word

A reaction, not a setup. The piece begins with you reacting *to writing the piece itself*.

> "Hooray!" — *Day 90: The One Simple Trick to Re-Designing Your Life*

> "Let me savor the moment a bit." — *Day 100: The 44 Books*

> "It's getting there." — *Day 98: What I Know for Sure*

> "O.k." — *Day 94: On Making an Argument*

> "Happy New Year!" — *Day 103: On Insecurity*

**When to use:** When the piece is celebratory, milestone-shaped, or self-aware. When you're posting Day 100 of a 100-day series. When the right opening is the *feeling of arriving*, not a story.

**Why it works:** Disarms the reader. Most blog posts open with effort; this one opens with breath. They'll keep reading just because the rhythm shifted.

**WARNING:** Only earns its place when the rest of the piece sustains the casual tone.

---

### Shape 8 — Topic setup / classroom

For the parts of a series. Establish the lineage of the post inside a sequence.

> "First, this is not about the film Black Swan starring Natalie Portman, but the book The Black Swan: The Impact of the Highly Improbable by Nassim Nicholas Taleb." — *Day 79: Design Thinking 3*

> "As the title suggests, I am going to talk about design in terms of heuristics and general probability." — *Day 80: Design Thinking 4*

> "So, let me tell you a bit about the background of this design thinking series." — *Day 78: Design Thinking 2*

> "In my previous blog On Design Thinking Part 1: The Gist, I have outlined the three key ideas of design thinking." — *Day 83: Design Thinking 5*

**When to use:** When the piece is part of a series. When the reader needs a moment of orientation to get the value out of what follows.

**Why it works:** Honest. No gimmicks. The reader knows where they are.

**Limitation:** Works only inside a series. As a one-off opening it's flat.

## Anti-patterns (NEVER do these)

These are the LLM-cliché shapes that show up when the model isn't given the bible. Banned:

- ❌ *"I remember standing in [place]…"*
- ❌ *"I was sitting on the bus when…"*
- ❌ *"It was a [season/weather] [time of day], and I…"*
- ❌ *"Imagine you are [doing thing]. Now imagine [twist]."*
- ❌ *"In a world where [observation], I want to talk about…"*
- ❌ *"Picture this: [scene]…"*
- ❌ *"Have you ever [done thing] and thought to yourself…?"*
- ❌ *"There's a [thing] I've been thinking about lately…"*

Any opening that starts with the writer-as-observer-of-themselves staging a scene. That's the Hollywood-LLM voice. Dr Non's voice is more direct, more social, more declarative.

## How to use this file

When drafting any first-person script in Dr Non's voice (podcast, wisdom video, blog post drafted by an LLM):

1. **Pick a shape from the list above before writing the first sentence.** Choose deliberately. Match shape to topic.
2. **Don't reuse the same shape twice in a row.** Rotate.
3. **Don't blend Shape 2 (confession) with Shape 6 (parable) in the opening** — pick one.
4. **Quote the example pattern back to the model** when prompting it. Example prompt fragment:

```
Open with Shape 3 — You-just-saw-something. Example shape:
"So, I'm [recent action] this [recent timeframe], and I [noticed
specific thing]." Then move into the topic. Do NOT use Shape 1
(People ask me) — used in the last piece.
```

5. **Cut the opening if it sounds like an LLM.** If the first sentence has "I remember", "I was standing", "imagine", "picture this", or "in a world where", delete it and try a different shape.

Keep a running log. After each piece, append:

```

| 2026-05-08 | 2     | Finding Motivation in Pain     |

```

This makes the rotation visible. If Shape 1 hasn't appeared in 6 pieces, push for it next.
